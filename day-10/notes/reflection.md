# Day 10 - Final Reflection & Learning Summary

## What I Learned
1. The structural difference between runtime processes (`ps`, `top`) and persistent background services (`systemctl`).
2. How systemd calendar timers and cron mechanisms act as primary persistence vectors on Linux systems.
3. The vital importance of separating raw empirical observations from analytical interpretations in incident reports.

## What I Can Demonstrate
* Execute process inspection queries (`ps -eo pid,ppid,user,stat,etime,comm`) and analyze parent-child hierarchies.
* Audit running services and enabled startup units using `systemctl`.
* Build, syntax-test (`bash -n`), and execute a read-only Bash automation script (`linux_triage.sh`).

## What Surprised Me
How much system telemetry is generated by default daemons on a standard Parrot OS boot, highlighting why narrow querying (`journalctl -u`, `ss -lnt`) is essential to avoid analyst fatigue.

## What I Could Not Establish
Whether background binaries on disk have been tampered with or modified, as a point-in-time triage snapshot cannot perform full file-integrity checksum verification.

## What I Would Investigate Next
Inspect the unit file paths and package ownership of any unfamiliar background services discovered during triage.

## What I Need to Practise
Advanced `journalctl` time-range filtering and regex parsing in shell scripts.

## Final Statement of Tool Capability
The `linux_triage.sh` script collected a limited point-in-time system snapshot of processes, services, timers, and listening ports to guide initial human investigation. No definitive conclusion about the overall security or malware-free status of the system can be made from this snapshot alone.
